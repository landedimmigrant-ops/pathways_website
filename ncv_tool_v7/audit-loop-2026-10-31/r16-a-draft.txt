1. A bridge-monitoring sensor network the provincial transport ministry uses to schedule inspections
   Provincial bridges are inspected on a fixed cycle, so a bridge that deteriorates between inspections can go unnoticed, and the ministry had no continuous measurements to rank which of its 2,400 structures needed attention first. I led the network design and the method that turns sensor data into inspection priorities, with my group building the sensing hardware and the ministry bridge engineers setting the alert rules. I wrote the identification algorithm and ran the field trials with the ministry crews. The work produced a network that now runs on 86 bridges, a damage-detection method, a dashboard used by ministry inspectors, two journal articles and three graduate theses. The ministry now uses the dashboard to rank bridges for inspection each quarter; inspectors visited 31 flagged structures in the first two years and found early cracking on nine, and two municipalities have asked to join the network. During the spring thaw of 2023 the network flagged a change in a rural bridge girder; the inspection two days later confirmed a failing bearing, and the ministry restricted heavy trucks until the repair. If the ministry extends the network to its 2,400 structures, inspection budgets could go first to the bridges that need them, and repairs could be scheduled before they become emergency closures. (a)

1. A national registry of cardiac arrest in the home: Resuscitation Outcomes Canada (ROC-Home)
   Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest kills about 35,000 Canadians a year and survival has hovered near 8% for two decades, but nobody could say which neighbourhoods, which dispatch protocols or which bystander programs explained the gaps between regions, because provinces kept their own incompatible records and none of them linked ambulance data to hospital discharge data for the same patient, which meant that every comparison was a comparison between systems that counted differently. I founded the registry, direct its steering group and negotiated the data-sharing agreements with 10 provincial health ministries. Linked paramedic, dispatch and discharge records for 212,000 arrests (2012–2023) in 10 provinces under one governance model; built the privacy-preserving linkage (≥99.2% match rate) with Statistics Canada and two health-system partners. Participating clinics (n = 120) were selected from 9 networks, e.g. the Alberta Primary Care Networks, Réseau de santé Vitalité and the Northern Ontario Family Health Teams, and were stratified by size and rurality (rural: <30,000 population) so that results would generalize to the settings where most older Canadians receive care; adults aged ≥65 y without known AF were invited at routine visits, fitted with a 14-day single-lead patch (Class II device) and followed for 12 months by linkage to provincial discharge data, and every episode ≥30 s was adjudicated blind to arm by three cardiologists including me, vs. the single-reader approach used in earlier trials. I wrote the grant, led the consortium of 31 investigators, set up the data-access terms with the device manufacturer (the company had no access to identifiable data and no veto over publication), and supervised the three postdoctoral fellows and four graduate students who carried out the analyses, including the cost-effectiveness model (incremental cost per QALY CAD$18,400; 95% CI 11,200–29,800). Two trial papers, a screening algorithm cleared by Health Canada (Class II licence) and a training course for 900 nurse practitioners. Detection of new AF rose from 1.6% to 4.8% (RR 3.0, 95% CI 2.6–3.4); 1,950 patients started anticoagulation. A 71-year-old woman in Sudbury had AF found at a flu-shot visit and has had no stroke since. A population screening program could prevent several hundred strokes a year in Canada. (a, b)

6. Genetic risk scores for familial hypercholesterolemia in a provincial health system
   Familial hypercholesterolemia affects 1 in 250 Canadians yet more than 90% are undiagnosed, so heart disease strikes at 40 instead of 70. I led the clinical genetics partnership and chaired the ethics and return-of-results committee. With the provincial laboratory network, we sequenced 60,000 volunteers drawn from the biobank (median age 58 y; 56% women), applied a 5-gene monogenic panel plus a 1.7-million-variant polygenic score, and returned results to 3,100 participants through lipid clinics under a return-of-results policy that I chaired, which was approved by three research ethics boards after 14 months of negotiation. The genomics company (a small start-up spun out of the university) supplied sequencing at cost and had no access to the clinical data, i.e. all linkage occurred inside the provincial secure environment; the score reached an AUC of 0.81 (95% CI 0.78–0.84) for LDL-C >4.9 mmol/L; and our cascade-screening pathway tested 1,100 first-degree relatives, 47% of whom were carriers vs. the 50% expected. Nature Medicine paper, a clinical-grade test and a cascade-screening pathway. 2,300 new diagnoses; cascade screening reached 1,100 relatives; LDL-C fell by a mean of 1.9 mmol/L. A father diagnosed through the program brought in his two daughters, aged 12 and 15. A national program could identify most affected families within ten years. (a, b)
