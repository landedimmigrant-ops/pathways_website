PERSONAL STATEMENT  [first in the CV, written last in the tool]
============================================================
I am a professor of medicine and clinician-scientist at a Canadian university and its affiliated cardiac institute. My work is in cardiovascular outcomes research in older adults.

For 25 years my research has asked why the cardiology evidence base describes patients who are not the ones in the clinic: adults older than 75, frail, or living far from a tertiary centre are under-represented in the trials that set our guidelines, so the people who carry most of the burden of heart disease receive care that was never tested on them. 1) pragmatic trials that include older and frail adults; 2) national registries that link ambulance, hospital and drug records, e.g. ROC-Home; 3) health-system partnerships (pharmaceutical, provincial ministries, networks of primary care clinics) that put the findings into practice. The thread is that every project begins with the patients who were left out. I trained in cardiology and clinical epidemiology (MD 1998; MSc 2004; FRCPC) and returned from a fellowship in Oxford in 2005 to a hospital that had no research infrastructure. A mentor’s question—“who is not in this trial?”—changed my program, and in 2010 I moved half of my clinical time to research. • H-index 62 (Scopus, 2026), >340 peer-reviewed papers (>40 as senior author in NEJM, Lancet, JAMA) • Principal investigator of 3 CIHR Foundation/Project Grants and 2 Heart & Stroke Foundation grants (total ≈ CAD$21M) • Chair, Canadian Cardiovascular Society guidelines panel 2021–2025 • Fellow, Canadian Academy of Health Sciences (2019). A Project Grant funds the next stage of this program, a pragmatic trial that tests frailty-guided care in a way that no single province could run alone, and the committee’s review criteria reward the partnerships I have built. The next five years will extend the frailty-stratified approach from NSTEMI to heart failure and atrial fibrillation and test it in community hospitals, with partners in four provinces and two Indigenous health authorities, if they wish to take part.

1. A national registry of cardiac arrest in the home: Resuscitation Outcomes Canada (ROC-Home)
   Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest kills about 35,000 Canadians a year and survival has hovered near 8% for two decades, but nobody could say which neighbourhoods, which dispatch protocols or which bystander programs explained the gaps between regions, because provinces kept their own incompatible records and none of them linked ambulance data to hospital discharge data for the same patient, which meant that every comparison was a comparison between systems that counted differently. I founded the registry, direct its steering group and negotiated the data-sharing agreements with 10 provincial health ministries. Linked paramedic, dispatch and discharge records for 212,000 arrests (2012–2023) in 10 provinces under one governance model; built the privacy-preserving linkage (≥99.2% match rate) with Statistics Canada and two health-system partners. A public dashboard (https://roc-home.example.ca); 22 papers, e.g. Nguyen L et al. Resuscitation 2022;170:45–53; a data standard now used by 9 provinces. Three provinces changed dispatch scripts after our regional comparison; Ontario funded 4,300 public defibrillators—survival in funded areas rose from 7.9% to 10.4% (adjusted OR 1.31, 95% CI 1.18–1.45). A paramedic crew in Moncton used the dashboard to argue for a new dispatch script and saved a teenager the following winter. A single national standard could let every Canadian health authority benchmark its arrest system against its peers. (a, b)
   a. Nguyen L, Roy M, et al. Linked national registry of out-of-hospital arrest. Resuscitation. 2022;170:45–53. doi:10.1016/j.resuscitation.2022.01.001  [Peer-reviewed]
   b. Ontario Ministry of Health. Public access defibrillator program evaluation, 2024. https://www.ontario.ca/pad-eval  [Policy / practice uptake]

2. Early invasive strategy vs. conservative care in older adults with NSTEMI—the SENIOR-ACS trial program (n = 4,812; 95% CI 0.71–0.89)
   In adults aged 75 years or older with non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), it was unknown whether routine early angiography improves outcomes vs. a selective approach, because trials had excluded the frail, i.e. the patients cardiologists see most often. Mortality at 1 year exceeds 20% in this group (e.g., 24.3 per 100 patient-years). I was the principal investigator, chaired the steering committee and wrote the protocol—the first to include frail patients (CFS ≥ 5) in an invasive-strategy trial. • Randomized 4,812 patients aged ≥75 y at 62 sites in 9 countries (1:1; early invasive within 24 h vs. selective) • Primary endpoint: death or MI at 12 months; HR 0.80 (95% CI 0.71–0.89; P < 0.001) • Frailty measured with the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS ≥5 in 38%); systolic BP ≥140 mmHg and LDL-C >2.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dL) recorded at baseline. 1. Primary results (Smith J, Tremblay A, et al. N Engl J Med. 2024;390:1123–1134. doi:10.1056/NEJMoa2400001).	2. Open frailty-stratified dataset, https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.1234567. 3. Five secondary papers, e.g. in JAMA Cardiol and Eur Heart J. The ESC and CCS guidelines now recommend early angiography in frail patients aged ≥75 y (Class IIa, 2025), i.e. practice changed in 11 countries; a provincial cardiac network in Alberta adopted our frailty pathway in “Cardiac Care Pathway 2.0”. In 2025 a 83-year-old man from Thunder Bay was offered angiography after our frailty score showed he was fit enough, and he returned to his farm. If the frailty-stratified approach is adopted in cardiac networks that serve the 1.2 million Canadians aged 75 or older, it could prevent an estimated 2,000 deaths or infarctions a year. (a, b, c)

3. Polypill therapy after myocardial infarction, tested in a pragmatic trial with a pharmaceutical partner (PILL-MI)
   Fewer than half of patients keep taking their four secondary-prevention drugs one year after a heart attack (adherence 48%, 95% CI 45–51%), and pill burden is a known, fixable cause. I designed the trial with the partner’s clinical team and held the academic data safety rules; the partner had no role in analysis. The trial was run as a pragmatic, registry-based, open-label study (see Fig. 2 of the protocol paper; Dr. Okafor chaired the DSMB) in which eligible patients (age ≥18 y; first MI within 6 months; eGFR >30 mL/min/1.73 m²) were randomized 1:1 by a central web system stratified by site, sex and prior revascularization; the polypill was manufactured and supplied at cost by the partner, i.e. no unrestricted grant was involved, whereas outcome ascertainment used linked administrative data (ICES, Manitoba Centre for Health Policy and the Quebec RAMQ) so that no patient had to attend an extra visit—and I personally negotiated each of the three data-access agreements, chaired the 14-member steering committee that met every month for six years, trained the 41 site investigators in two in-person workshops (Toronto 2019; Vancouver 2021), wrote the statistical analysis plan with our biostatistician (Prof. L. Chen) before unblinding, and presented the interim safety review (systolic BP <100 mmHg in 2.1% vs. 1.9%; hazard ratio 1.09, 95% CI 0.82–1.44) to the independent committee, which recommended continuation without modification. Primary paper in Lancet (Okafor C, et al., 2023); an open statistical analysis plan; a cost-effectiveness model used by two provincial formulary committees. Adherence at 24 months 71% vs. 49%; the polypill was added to the Ontario and British Columbia formularies in 2024. If every post-infarction patient in Canada were offered the polypill, we estimate about 3,000 fewer readmissions a year. (a, b, c, d)
   a. Okafor C, Lee S, et al. Polypill after myocardial infarction. Lancet. 2023;401:112–121. doi:10.1016/S0140-6736(23)00001-X  [Peer-reviewed]
   b. Registered protocol: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00000000  [Dataset / tool]
   c. Provincial formulary decisions, 2024 (Ontario; British Columbia)  [Policy / practice uptake]
   d. Open analysis plan, OSF: https://osf.io/abcde/  [Dataset / tool]

4. Women’s heart health: sex-specific risk prediction and the WHEART cohort
   Women are under-diagnosed with coronary disease, and risk scores built mostly on men misclassify them. I led the cohort and its industry partnership; my group built the prediction model. The WHEART cohort enrolled 30,000 women across 14 sites between 2010 and 2022, and I will describe it at length here because the history matters: we began in 2010 with a single-centre pilot funded by a hospital foundation, which we expanded in 2013 with CIHR funding to four sites, then to fourteen after the 2017 partnership with a diagnostics company that supplied imaging core-lab support, and every step required a new ethics agreement, a new data-sharing model and a new group of local coordinators who had to be trained, retained and supported through two changes of electronic record system, a pandemic and a ransomware incident that shut the central server for eleven days in 2021, during which we reconstructed the cohort database from site-level backups with a loss of less than 0.1% of records. Model published in Circulation (Haddad N, et al., 2024); the score is in the “Canadian Women’s Heart Calculator”; 31 papers; an open codebook. Used in 210 clinics; reclassified 14% of women to a higher-risk category (NRI 0.14, 95% CI 0.10–0.18). A 58-year-old woman in Regina whose chest pain had been dismissed twice was reclassified to high risk by the calculator and had a stent placed within a week. The calculator could reach every woman having a cardiovascular risk assessment in Canada. (a, b, c, d)

5. Wearable atrial fibrillation screening in primary care: the AF-SCREEN program
   About one in three strokes in people over 65 follows atrial fibrillation that was never detected. I led the clinical arm and negotiated the device supply and the data-access terms with the manufacturer. Participating clinics (n = 120) were selected from 9 networks, e.g. the Alberta Primary Care Networks, Réseau de santé Vitalité and the Northern Ontario Family Health Teams, and were stratified by size and rurality (rural: <30,000 population) so that results would generalize to the settings where most older Canadians receive care; adults aged ≥65 y without known AF were invited at routine visits, fitted with a 14-day single-lead patch (Class II device) and followed for 12 months by linkage to provincial discharge data, and every episode ≥30 s was adjudicated blind to arm by three cardiologists including me, vs. the single-reader approach used in earlier trials. I wrote the grant, led the consortium of 31 investigators, set up the data-access terms with the device manufacturer (the company had no access to identifiable data and no veto over publication), and supervised the three postdoctoral fellows and four graduate students who carried out the analyses, including the cost-effectiveness model (incremental cost per QALY CAD$18,400; 95% CI 11,200–29,800). Two trial papers, a screening algorithm cleared by Health Canada (Class II licence) and a training course for 900 nurse practitioners. Detection of new AF rose from 1.6% to 4.8% (RR 3.0, 95% CI 2.6–3.4); 1,950 patients started anticoagulation. A 71-year-old woman in Sudbury had AF found at a flu-shot visit and has had no stroke since. A population screening program could prevent several hundred strokes a year in Canada. (a, b)
   a. Tran K, et al. Patch-based AF screening in primary care. JAMA. 2022;327:2210–2219.  [Peer-reviewed]
   b. Health Canada medical device licence 123456, 2023.  [Policy / practice uptake]

6. Genetic risk scores for familial hypercholesterolemia in a provincial health system
   Familial hypercholesterolemia affects 1 in 250 Canadians yet more than 90% are undiagnosed, so heart disease strikes at 40 instead of 70. I led the clinical genetics partnership and chaired the ethics and return-of-results committee. With the provincial laboratory network, we sequenced 60,000 volunteers drawn from the biobank (median age 58 y; 56% women), applied a 5-gene monogenic panel plus a 1.7-million-variant polygenic score, and returned results to 3,100 participants through lipid clinics under a return-of-results policy that I chaired, which was approved by three research ethics boards after 14 months of negotiation. The genomics company (a small start-up spun out of the university) supplied sequencing at cost and had no access to the clinical data, i.e. all linkage occurred inside the provincial secure environment; the score reached an AUC of 0.81 (95% CI 0.78–0.84) for LDL-C >4.9 mmol/L; and our cascade-screening pathway tested 1,100 first-degree relatives, 47% of whom were carriers vs. the 50% expected. Nature Medicine paper, a clinical-grade test and a cascade-screening pathway. 2,300 new diagnoses; cascade screening reached 1,100 relatives; LDL-C fell by a mean of 1.9 mmol/L. A father diagnosed through the program brought in his two daughters, aged 12 and 15. A national program could identify most affected families within ten years. (a, b)
   a. Patel R, et al. Polygenic and monogenic FH detection. Nat Med. 2024;30:211–220.  [Peer-reviewed]
   b. Provincial FH pathway, 2024.  [Policy / practice uptake]
