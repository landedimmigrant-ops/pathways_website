PERSONAL STATEMENT  [first in the CV, written last in the tool]
============================================================
I am a professor of civil engineering at a Canadian university. My work is in geotechnical engineering for roads on permafrost.

My program takes up the question of how northern highways can be built and maintained as the ground under them thaws, a question the territorial Department of Transportation and the communities along its corridors raised with me. Three strands address it: a ground-temperature monitoring network run with the department, a design guide the department adopted as its standard, and a training program for community monitors in Inuit and First Nations communities. All three put the measurements and the decisions in the hands of the people who maintain the road. I came to this work from a consulting practice in foundation design, where I saw embankments fail for lack of local data. I returned to graduate school in 2011 to study heat flow in embankments and have worked with the department since 2016. I have held two Discovery Grants and an Alliance grant with the department, supervised 35 trainees including 19 community monitors, and chaired the committee that drafted the territory's embankment standard. The guide is cited in the 2024 provincial and territorial road design review. Discovery funds the long program of field measurement that the monitoring network depends on, and a Discovery Grant lets me keep the measurement record independent of any single project. Next I will extend the network to embankments built on ice-rich silts, where current models underestimate settlement, and hand the readings to the communities as they are collected.

MOST SIGNIFICANT CONTRIBUTIONS AND EXPERIENCES
============================================================
1. A ground-temperature network that tells a territorial highway crew where the road is thawing
   The territorial Department of Transportation maintained 410 kilometres of gravel highway on permafrost with no way of knowing which embankments were losing frozen ground until the surface failed, so repairs came after closures instead of before them. The department asked for a monitoring approach it could run with its own staff. I designed the sensor layout and the thaw-depth analysis and led the field program; the department staff installed and maintained the stations, and a colleague at another university led the remote-sensing component. With the department we placed ground-temperature strings at 46 embankment sites over six years, 2019 to 2025, and trained four department technicians to read and service them. We combined the readings with annual thaw-depth probing and a numerical model of heat flow through the embankment. The work produced an open ground-temperature dataset of 3.1 million readings, a thaw-risk ranking method, and three journal papers, two co-authored with department engineers. The department now schedules embankment repairs from the ranking and has taken over the network; unplanned closures at monitored sites fell from 9 in 2018 to 2 in 2025. Two neighbouring jurisdictions have asked to adopt the station design. The same monitoring approach could support design decisions for all-season roads in other northern jurisdictions, and the department decides whether stations are added at the remaining 60 sites. 2. A design guide that lets northern highway engineers design for thaw
   Road engineers in the territory designed embankments with southern Canadian methods that assume the ground stays frozen, and the department needed design rules for a warming climate that its own staff could apply without a specialist. I wrote the guide with two colleagues; I led the thermal design chapters and the case studies, and the department engineers set the format and the review process. We drew on 14 case-study embankments and on field tests at three sites, and held 6 review workshops with department engineers and contractors in 2 communities. The work produced a 120-page design guide, a spreadsheet design tool and a short course for practitioners. The department adopted the guide as its standard for new embankments in 2024, and 5 highway projects have been designed with it so far. If the territorial standard is adopted into the national code for northern roads, the guide could reach every jurisdiction with permafrost; that decision rests with the standards committee. (a, b)

3. Community monitors who read the ground temperatures in their own region
   Inuit and First Nations communities along the highway corridors asked to read the thaw data for the land around their communities themselves, because the numbers reached them late and without context. I co-designed the training with two community education leads and taught the technical modules; the community leads chose where the monitors worked and what is shared outside the community. From 2021 to 2025 we ran 5 training sessions in 4 communities, with 19 community monitors reading and servicing 12 stations on their own lands, supported by 2 graduate students. The work produced a bilingual field manual in English and Inuktitut, a community-held data protocol, and a monitor certification the department accepts as a qualification. Four communities now hold and report their own station data to the department, and 6 monitors have been hired as part-time department technicians. The certification could be offered to other communities along northern highways, and each community decides whether and how its data is used. (a)
