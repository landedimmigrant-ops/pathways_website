PERSONAL STATEMENT  [first in the CV, written last in the tool]
============================================================
I am an associate professor of history at a mid-sized Canadian university. My work is in nineteenth-century social history and digital editing.

How do ordinary rural households, and the women in them, appear in the records they kept themselves, and how can digital methods make those records usable by scholars and the public? Three strands: a digital edition of a 19th-century diary, a co-authored study of household account books, and an undergraduate research program. The thread is that ordinary people kept records, and scholarship should be able to use them. I came to the diary through a graduate seminar in paleography, and moved from archival work into digital editing after a two-year postdoctoral fellowship in a humanities computing lab. A monograph with a university press, a digital edition of 1,400 pages that 30 researchers have used, two edited special journal issues, and a postdoctoral fellowship. The Insight Grant funds a sustained program of research of three to five years, which is the right scale to extend the edition method to two further diaries with a team. Next I plan to edit two more diaries from the same district and link the three editions so that readers can follow one household across decades.

MOST SIGNIFICANT CONTRIBUTIONS AND EXPERIENCES
============================================================
1. Digital edition of the Harlow diary, 1851 to 1879
   A 28-year private diary describes a rural household and its correspondents but has been available only as an uncatalogued manuscript, so historians could not cite or search it. I led the editorial team and wrote the transcription rules for the diary. We transcribed 1,400 diary pages from 28 notebooks held in two archives, encoded them in TEI XML with a team of 3 research assistants and 2 librarians, and built a searchable site. A public digital edition with 1,400 pages of transcription, a name and place index of about 600 entries, and an open-access dataset. Thirty researchers and 4 local history societies have cited or used the edition since its launch in 2023, and a county museum used it to rewrite its exhibition text. A retired schoolteacher in the village where the diarist lived found her great-grandmother named in the 1862 entries and brought the pages to a school assembly. Other 19th-century diaries could be encoded with the same method, and teachers could use the edition in schools. (a, b)

2. Co-authored book on women and household accounts in rural Ontario
   Historians have described farm women as unpaid helpers, but household account books suggest that they managed credit and trade in the district, and no study has used them together. I co-wrote four of the seven chapters and the framing chapters, with two co-authors. We drew on 41 household account books from six townships, read 2,300 entries, and compared them with census returns, in a team of three authors. A scholarly monograph of 320 pages with eleven tables, published by a university press in 2022, and a related journal article. Fifteen scholars have cited it, and two history courses assigned it in 2023 and 2024. A regional archive reorganized its account-book holdings after the book appeared. Other regions could be re-examined with account books, which might change how credit in farm households is understood. (a)
   a. Marlow, Okafor and Reyes. Ledgers and Looms: Farm Women and Credit in Rural Ontario. University Press, 2022. 320 pp.  [Peer-reviewed]
