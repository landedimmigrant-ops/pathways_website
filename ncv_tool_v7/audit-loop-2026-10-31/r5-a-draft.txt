I am an assistant professor of sociology at Made-up University. My work is in housing, tenancy and community-engaged sociology.

Tenants in older rental buildings are displaced by renovations that no public record counts, and the people most affected hold the least information about how common this is. 1. Counting renovation-based evictions (linked permit and tribunal records) 2. Plain-language materials written with tenants 3. Open data that tenants’ associations and councils can reuse The thread: tenants hold evidence that public records leave out. I grew up in a walk-up building that was emptied for renovation, and I trained in survey methods during my postdoc; I came back to this city to work with the association that organized my neighbours. A 2025 article in the Journal of Housing Studies (23 citations), an open dataset of 412 buildings downloaded 740 times, a Canadian Housing Research Network data award & a community report in three languages. Next I will repeat the linkage in two more provinces, with tenants’ associations leading the questions.

PERSONAL STATEMENT  [first in the CV, written last in the tool]
============================================================
I am a professor of environmental engineering at a large Canadian research university. My work is in urban water systems and the control of stormwater and wastewater infrastructure.

For 20 years my research has returned to one problem: cities and utilities discharge more polluted water to rivers than their infrastructure and budgets were designed for, and the cost falls on downstream communities. Three strands address it: real-time control of combined sewers, low-cost treatment of landfill leachate and open models and monitoring for river nutrients. In each the control, the measurement or the model is built with the operator who will use it. I trained as a hydraulic engineer and spent four years as a consulting engineer, which is why I start from the operating decision a utility faces. I have held two consecutive Discovery Grants, hold an NSERC industrial research chair and have trained 64 people. A provincial ministry and a regional utility have adopted methods from my group. This renewal funds the long-term work that the partnerships depend on, and the next phase needs five more years of continuous data. The next phase extends the control methods to combined and separate systems in cold regions, and the monitoring methods to lakes.

MOST SIGNIFICANT CONTRIBUTIONS AND EXPERIENCES

1. Real-time control cut sewer overflows by 62 percent in three Quebec municipalities
   Combined sewer systems in older Quebec towns discharge untreated overflow into rivers during storms. A ministry of environment fines the municipalities, and a regional water utility must meet a 2030 overflow limit with a fixed capital budget. Residents downstream fish and swim in these rivers. I directed the project from the first pilot, designed the control algorithm and supervised the field trials in all three municipalities. My group built sensors and a model-predictive controller for 41 regulator gates, tested it in a hydraulic lab, then ran it live for two storm seasons in Granby, Saint-Hyacinthe and Drummondville with the regional water utility, using 9 years of the utility flow records. The work produced a control software package, 14 refereed papers, a design guide adopted by the ministry and the doctoral theses of three trainees. Combined sewer overflows in the three towns fell by 62 percent over two storm seasons. The ministry now accepts real-time control as an alternative to new storage tanks, and the utility has avoided 18 million dollars in tank construction. Other municipalities with combined sewers could meet their overflow limits at a fraction of the capital cost, and rivers downstream could reopen for swimming and fishing during the warm season. (a, b)
   a. Tremblay, M., and Okafor, A. (2024). Model-predictive control of combined sewer regulators. Water Research 251, 120-134.  [Peer-reviewed]

   b. Fault-free overflow control software v2.1, released under an open licence with the 41-gate calibration data.  [Dataset / tool]

2. An open stormwater model calibrated on 40 Canadian catchments
   Municipal engineers size stormwater pipes with models that were calibrated on European storms, and the Canadian winter and snowmelt events break their assumptions. Utilities and the ministry need one shared model they can audit. I contributed the snowmelt and frozen-ground module and calibrated it against 12 of the catchments. Six universities and four municipal utilities pooled 40 catchments of rain, snowmelt and flow data, and I ran the snowmelt calibration with two graduate students over four winters. The model is released as open code with a 40-catchment benchmark dataset and 6 refereed papers. Nine municipalities now size new storm sewers with the model, and the ministry lists it among the accepted tools in its stormwater design manual. Climate adaptation planning for stormwater could use one audited model, which would make design standards comparable across provinces. (a, b)

3. A passive biochar filter removed 91 percent of PFAS from landfill leachate
   Landfill leachate carries PFAS into groundwater, and municipal operators have no treatment that fits a small site budget. A provincial ministry is drafting limits for these compounds, and the operators need a method that works before the limits take effect. I co-led the work with a materials chemist and designed the column tests and the field monitoring plan. We screened 12 biochars in column tests, built a pilot filter at two landfill sites and sampled leachate monthly for 30 months with the ministry laboratory. The work produced a pilot filter design, 8 refereed papers, a sampling protocol and two master theses. The ministry laboratory now uses our sampling protocol, and 5 landfill operators have installed the filter. Small operators across Canada could treat leachate on site for roughly one tenth of the cost of hauling it to a treatment plant. (a, b)

4. A river nutrient monitoring network that now sets provincial licence limits
   Algal blooms in three watersheds closed drinking water intakes in 2018 and 2019, and the ministry had too few measurements to say which discharges caused them. Downstream towns, a hydroelectric utility and farmers all bore the cost. I led the network design, trained the field crews and wrote the load-estimation method the ministry adopted. We placed 64 continuous sensors on 11 rivers, combined them with weekly grab samples for five years and trained a load model that separates farm, urban and treatment-plant sources. The network produced a public nutrient data portal, the load-estimation method, 10 refereed papers and four doctoral theses. The ministry now writes phosphorus limits into licences from our load estimates, and the hydroelectric utility uses the portal to plan reservoir releases during bloom season. Any province with sparse river data could set evidence-based limits within one monitoring cycle and avoid costly intake closures. (a, b)

THE CANDIDATE'S BACKGROUND AND SKILLS / PARCOURS ET COMPÉTENCES DE LA PERSONNE CANDIDATE  [first in the CV, written last in the tool]
============================================================
I am a research-creation artist at a Montréal university. My work is in sound and media art.

How can the everyday sounds of a neighbourhood be kept, shared and heard in places where residents are not usually invited to listen? My work asks what a community loses when its sounds leave no record, and how an artwork can keep them in residents’ hands. Three strands of work address this question: an installation based on recordings made by residents, a performance created with a neighbourhood, and a printed artist’s book that carries sound to readers. In all three, residents choose what is kept, and I compose the final form. I trained in sound composition, then moved to research-creation after a residency in a community radio station in 2019, where residents asked me to record their lane rather than a stage. I have toured one installation in seven venues, directed a performance with 42 residents and published an artist’s book held by four libraries, supported by two grants from the Conseil des arts et des lettres du Québec. The program funds research-creation developed with partners outside the university. All three works were made with residents, and the program’s objective to open creation to communities is where my record is strongest. In this team I bring the field recordings, the composition and the relationship with the neighbourhood organizations in Hochelaga-Maisonneuve. The other members bring the performance direction, the community research and the publication design that I lack. Over the next three years I will take the installation to six boroughs in 2,5 times as many venues and test whether residents can run the recording sessions without me.

MOST SIGNIFICANT CONTRIBUTIONS AND EXPERIENCES / CONTRIBUTIONS ET EXPÉRIENCES LES PLUS IMPORTANTES
============================================================
1. A touring sound installation on Montréal’s vanishing back lanes
   Montréal’s ruelles are being paved, fenced or redeveloped, and the sound of lane life has no record in public archives or in art history. Residents of Hochelaga and Saint-Henri had no way to keep it. I conceived, composed and toured the installation myself, with 11 residents of the neighbourhood who recorded their own lanes. Over 2,5 years I recorded 140 hours of lane sound in nine lanes, composed an eight-channel piece from the material residents chose to keep and built a 6 m installation that I installed myself in each venue. The installation « Voix de ruelle », shown in seven venues in Québec between 2024 and 2026, and an open online archive of the recordings. Two boroughs, Le Sud-Ouest and Verdun, cited the recordings in their lane-greening consultations, and about 3 200 visitors attended, 40 % of them for the first time at a contemporary art venue. The archive could become a regular source of information for consultations on public space in Québec and in other cities. (a, b)

2. A community performance co-created with the residents of Hochelaga-Maisonneuve
   Neighbourhood residents rarely take part in the making of contemporary performance, and the stories of the 1970s housing struggles in Hochelaga-Maisonneuve were told by outsiders. I co-led the project with the community organization Les Voisins du parc, and I directed the staging. Over 14 months I led 30 workshops with 42 residents, then directed a 60-minute performance created from their texts, songs and objects, which we presented in a church hall and in two parks. The performance « Les Voisins du parc », given 12 times between 2023 and 2025 to about 1 800 spectators, and a documentation booklet that the residents wrote. The organization has run the workshops every winter since 2024, and a municipal library now lends the booklet. The method could be adopted by other neighbourhoods that want to make their own history public. (a, b)

3. A published artist’s book on the soundscape of a Québec village
   Villages along the Saint-Laurent are losing the daily sounds of church bells, ferries and markets, and no print form existed to carry sound to readers who cannot visit an exhibition. I wrote, scored and designed the book on my own, and worked with a printer from Trois-Rivières on the paper and the binding. Over 18 months I recorded the village in four seasons, transcribed the sounds as graphic scores and designed a 96-page book of 1,5 kg with a QR code that opens each recording. The artist’s book « Le village à l’oreille », 500 copies, published by Les Éditions du Ressac in 2025. Three university libraries and the Bibliothèque et Archives nationales du Québec hold the book, and a school in the village uses it in its music classes. The book could help other villages record their soundscapes in print. (a)
