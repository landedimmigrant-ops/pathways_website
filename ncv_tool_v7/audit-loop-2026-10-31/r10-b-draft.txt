PERSONAL STATEMENT  [first in the CV, written last in the tool]
============================================================
I am a professor of civil engineering at a Canadian university. My work is in structural health monitoring of bridges.

Bridges deteriorate between inspections, and a province cannot see the change until the next visit. My program turns measurements from sensors on in-service bridges into decisions about which structure to inspect next. Three strands: sensor networks that collect data on in-service bridges; methods that separate damage from the effects of weather and traffic; and open data that lets other groups test those methods. The thread is that each method is tested on bridges a ministry manages. I came to this work from a bridge design office, where I saw an inspection cycle miss a failing bearing. That is why my group tests each method against documented repairs. I hold the NSERC Discovery Grant I am renewing and two industry-partnered grants. The ministry has used my network for six years, my open dataset has 1,900 downloads, and I have trained 65 students, many of whom now work in industry and government. The Discovery Grant funds the long-term work that ministry contracts do not, such as the open dataset and the methods research; my industry partners fund the field deployments. Next I will extend the network from bridges to rail structures and tunnels, where the ministry and a transit agency have offered sites, and test whether the method works with fewer sensors per structure.

MOST SIGNIFICANT CONTRIBUTIONS AND EXPERIENCES
============================================================
1. A bridge-monitoring sensor network the provincial transport ministry uses to schedule inspections
   Provincial bridges are inspected on a fixed cycle, so a bridge that deteriorates between inspections can go unnoticed, and the ministry had no continuous measurements to rank which of its 2,400 structures needed attention first. I led the network design and the method that turns sensor data into inspection priorities, with my group building the sensing hardware and the ministry bridge engineers setting the alert rules. We instrumented 86 highway and municipal bridges with accelerometers and strain gauges, collected vibration records for six years, and tested the damage-detection method against 12 documented repairs and three load tests. I wrote the identification algorithm and ran the field trials with the ministry crews. The work produced a network that now runs on 86 bridges, a damage-detection method, a dashboard used by ministry inspectors, two journal articles and three graduate theses. The ministry now uses the dashboard to rank bridges for inspection each quarter; inspectors visited 31 flagged structures in the first two years and found early cracking on nine, and two municipalities have asked to join the network. During the spring thaw of 2023 the network flagged a change in a rural bridge girder; the inspection two days later confirmed a failing bearing, and the ministry restricted heavy trucks until the repair. If the ministry extends the network to its 2,400 structures, inspection budgets could go first to the bridges that need them, and repairs could be scheduled before they become emergency closures. (a)
   a. Ministry of Transportation adopted the sensor-network dashboard into its quarterly bridge-inspection planning (Bridge Management Directive 2022-04), with 86 bridges reporting.  [Policy / practice uptake]
      Inspectors now visit the bridges the dashboard ranks highest, which replaced a fixed two-year cycle for those structures.

2. An open dataset of bridge vibration records that other groups use to test damage-detection methods
   Researchers who develop methods for monitoring bridges have few public, long-term vibration records from structures in service, so methods are tested on laboratory specimens or simulated data and their results are hard to compare. I designed the data collection and the release format, and I curate the dataset with a data steward at the university library. We cleaned and documented six years of records from 86 bridges, matched them to the inspection and repair history the ministry shared, and removed information that identifies a structure or its location. The work produced an open dataset of 4.1 billion samples with documentation, a validation script and a DOI, and a data article describing it. The dataset has been downloaded 1,900 times by 41 institutions, and it has been used in two benchmark studies and four graduate courses. A common benchmark could let research groups compare damage-detection methods on real bridge data and shorten the path from a new method to field use. (a)
   a. Bridge Vibration Records, version 2.1, held by the university library data repository, open access under a CC BY licence, doi:10.5683/SP3/EXAMPLE  [Dataset / tool]
      Six years of acceleration and strain records from 86 in-service bridges, used by research groups in 12 countries.

3. A damage-detection method that separates a failing bridge bearing from the effects of temperature and traffic
   Vibration records from a bridge change with temperature and traffic load, and existing methods raised so many false alarms that the ministry inspectors stopped trusting them. I developed the method with two doctoral students, and a ministry engineer defined the thresholds that count as a warning. We trained and tested the method on six years of records from 86 bridges and checked it against 12 documented repairs and three controlled load tests. The method is described in a peer-reviewed article, implemented in an open-source package and used in the ministry dashboard. The method cut false alarms by 71 percent on the test bridges, and the inspectors who tested it now act on its warnings. The same approach could apply to rail bridges, tunnels and other structures where load and weather change the signal. (a)
   a. Okafor, A., Lindqvist, M., and Tran, P. (2024). Separating bearing damage from environmental variation in bridge vibration records. Journal of Bridge Engineering, 29(3), 04024012.  [Peer-reviewed]
      Reports a 71 percent drop in false alarms across 86 in-service bridges.
