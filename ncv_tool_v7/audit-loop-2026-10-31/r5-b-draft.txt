PERSONAL STATEMENT  [first in the CV, written last in the tool]
============================================================
I am a professor at a research university. My work is in community-governed health research with First Nations and Métis communities.

Health planning for First Nations and Métis communities has been done about them, with indicators and data that outside agencies define and hold, and the communities’ own priorities do not appear in the plans. Three strands share one thread, community authority over research: a community-owned health survey, a land-based youth program evaluated with the Nation that runs it, and a report co-authored with community members that a health authority adopted. In each, the communities set the questions, hold the data and approve what is published. I joined a health survey in a northern community as a research assistant, where an Elder asked who would keep the data and no one on the team could answer. That question changed how I design studies, and I have worked under community research protocols since then. Two SSHRC Insight Grants and a CIHR Project Grant since 2016; 38 peer-reviewed papers, 11 co-authored with community members; and an invitation from a partner Nation’s Elders’ council to sit on its research review committee in 2024. Partnership Grants fund research that partners lead alongside universities, which is how this work is organized. The grant would fund a second phase with the health authority and six communities as partners, and the communities would hold the data throughout. The next phase extends the survey to youth and to Métis communities, with each community deciding at the start whether and how its data are shared.

MOST SIGNIFICANT CONTRIBUTIONS AND EXPERIENCES
============================================================
1. A health survey that six First Nations communities own, govern and interpret
   Health surveys of First Nations and Métis communities have usually been designed, analysed and held by outside agencies, and the communities had little say in which questions were asked or who could use the answers. I co-lead the survey’s methods with the partner communities’ health directors, who set the questions, hold the data and decide what is published. From 2019 to 2024 community researchers surveyed 1,850 adults in six communities under OCAP® principles, and I trained them in interviewing and consent. The communities’ data governance committee reviewed every analysis before it left the community, and the data stay on servers run by the communities’ health organization. The partnership produced a community-owned survey instrument, a data governance agreement, a plain-language report in two languages and four articles co-authored with community members, each approved by the data governance committee before submission. The provincial health authority revised its Indigenous health indicators in 2025 to use the measures the communities defined, and two other First Nations adopted the survey instrument in 2026. Community members now deliver the interviewer training that I led in 2019. At the 2024 community gathering an Elder read the first results aloud in her own language, and the youth program team asked to use the findings to plan the next season. Community-owned surveys could make community governance of data a condition of health planning across the province, and the same protocols could carry over to Métis and Inuit partners. (a, b)

2. A land-based youth program, evaluated with the Nation that runs it
   Programs for Indigenous youth are often evaluated by outside teams with measures that leave out what the community counts as well-being, and funders and families cannot see what the programs do. The Nation’s education department directs the program, and I lead the evaluation design with its Elders’ council, who define what counts as success. From 2021 to 2024 the Nation ran 14 land-based camps for 210 youth aged 12 to 17. I worked with the Elders’ council to set the evaluation measures in the community’s language, and community researchers collected stories and attendance records that the council reviewed each season. The evaluation produced a community-defined well-being framework, three seasonal reports to the council, a facilitator guide and two co-authored articles, one led by a community researcher. The Nation renewed the program’s funding for five years in 2025 using the framework, and the provincial education ministry asked to review the framework for use with two other First Nations schools. In 2024 a youth participant presented the results to the council and chose which photographs appear in the report. An evaluation built on community-defined well-being could change how governments fund land-based programs for Indigenous youth. (a, b)
   a. A. Author, B. Author (community researcher, first author) and C. Author (2025). Measuring well-being on the land: a framework defined by the Nation’s Elders’ council. Journal of Indigenous Health Research, 12(2), 45–62.  [Peer-reviewed]
      The first author, a community researcher, presented the framework to the council before submission.
   b. Five-year renewal of the Nation’s land-based program funding (2025), awarded on the basis of the community-defined framework  [Funding]

3. A community-authored report that changed how a provincial health authority plans Indigenous health services
   Provincial health plans for First Nations and Métis communities have relied on indicators the health authority defined, and community members could not see their own priorities in them. I convene the writing group of four community members and two researchers and write its methods and synthesis chapters; the community members decide the report’s findings and recommendations. In 2023 the writing group met 18 times in four communities to write a plain-language report from the survey results and from 40 conversations with Elders, youth and health workers who agreed to be quoted. Each person checked every quotation attributed to them before it was printed. The group produced a 90-page report in English and in the two languages of the partner communities, a one-page summary for each council and 12 recommendations, co-authored with six community members. The health authority adopted nine of the 12 recommendations in its 2025 Indigenous health plan and now reports yearly against the community-defined indicators. Two regional health boards asked the group to present the report in 2026. At the 2025 launch the council chair presented the report to the authority’s board, which approved the first three recommendations that afternoon. The report’s approach could change how health authorities in other provinces set indicators with Indigenous communities. (a)
   a. Provincial Indigenous Health Plan 2025–2030, section 4: nine of the 12 recommendations adopted, with annual reporting against community-defined indicators  [Policy / practice uptake]
      The plan names the community writing group as the source of the indicators.
