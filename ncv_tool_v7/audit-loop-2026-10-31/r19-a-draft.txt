PERSONAL STATEMENT  [first in the CV, written last in the tool]
============================================================
I am an independent media artist and research-creation scholar at Atelier Fil (fictional), an artist-run production space in Montreal. My work is in interactive documentary and sound installation.

My work asks what a place sounds like when the people who made it speak for themselves, and what a public archive owes the voices it keeps. Three strands carry the question: an interactive documentary built with former mill workers, a sound installation made from municipal archive recordings and a free workshop series that teaches teenagers to record their neighbourhood. The thread is listening as a way of making a record. I trained in film editing and worked for six years as a sound editor on other people's documentaries before a master's in research-creation, where I learned to treat the recordings themselves as the subject. A summer residency in an archive led me to the mill. My films have screened at two festivals in 2025, and the linear cut of the documentary won an audience award. The sound installation drew about 2,300 visitors and was reviewed in a national art magazine. A book chapter on the workshop method appeared in an edited volume in 2025. The Insight Development Grant funds early-stage research that tests a new direction, and my record shows a documentary, an installation and a workshop series that now need a shared method. I bring the making skills, the archive relationships and a published account of the teaching method. The next phase builds a shared listening method across the three strands and tests it with two other municipal archives.

MOST SIGNIFICANT CONTRIBUTIONS AND EXPERIENCES
============================================================
1. An interactive documentary that gives a closed textile mill back its workers' voices
   Documentaries about closed mills show the machinery and the ruins, and the sound of the work, as the women and men who ran the looms described it, is missing from the record. I wanted to ask what a place sounds like when only its workers can describe it. I directed, shot, edited and programmed the documentary on my own; a sound designer and a web developer each worked on one part for pay. Over 26 months I recorded 41 hours of interviews with 19 former mill workers, digitized 300 photographs from two family collections, and built a browsable web documentary in which each worker's account is tied to a room of the mill. I composed the navigation and wrote the code for the interface. The project produced a 90-minute interactive documentary with 19 linked accounts, a public web edition and a 12-minute linear cut for festivals. Two film festivals screened the work in 2025, and the linear cut won the audience award for best documentary short at one of them. A former mill worker told me after a screening that she had never heard her own loom described by anyone else, and the town historical society now plays the recordings in its permanent display. At the closing screening in the former mill canteen, three retired weavers stood up one after another to name the machines in the footage. If other regional archives adopt the room-by-room structure, local oral history could be presented as a place that visitors walk through, and community museums could share the same open code to publish their own collections. 2. A sound installation built from forty years of municipal archive recordings
   Municipal sound archives hold council meetings and radio spots that few people ever hear, and their grain and silences carry a record of the town that the transcripts leave out. I asked how an installation could let visitors listen to a town talking to itself. I composed the piece, edited the recordings and built the speaker array myself; an audio engineer tuned the room for two days. Over 14 months I catalogued 612 reels in the municipal archive, selected 90 minutes of speech, and arranged them across a 16-speaker array in a disused loading bay, where each speaker plays one decade. The work produced a 16-channel sound installation, a catalogue of the 612 reels and a 24-page printed listening guide. The installation ran for six weeks in 2025 and drew about 2,300 visitors. A critical review in a national art magazine described it as the most precise use of archival speech in the city that year, and the municipal archive has since asked to deposit my edited selection with its collection. On the closing weekend, a retired council clerk stood under the speaker for 1978 and recognized her own voice reading the minutes. If the archive adopts the listening guide, other municipal archives could open their audio holdings to artists and teachers, and public listening could become part of how a town reads its own records. 3. A free workshop series that teaches teenagers to record and edit their neighbourhood's stories
   Teenagers in post-industrial neighbourhoods are often the subject of documentaries and seldom the makers of them. I asked what changes in a community archive when the young people who live there hold the microphone. I designed the curriculum and taught all sessions; two undergraduates helped run equipment, and the library hosted the room. Across three years I ran 11 workshops for 46 teenagers aged 13 to 17 at a public library, teaching field recording, interviewing and sound editing, and publishing their finished pieces in a shared online listening room. The series produced 52 teen-made audio pieces, a printed workshop manual and a book chapter on the teaching method. Eleven participants have returned for a second year, and four now volunteer as assistants. The library has made the series part of its permanent youth programming and has put the manual on its shelves. If the manual is taken up by other libraries, young people in other districts could build local sound archives, and community history projects could count teenagers as authors of the record. (a, b)

[One thing you do] Before any session I ask each teenager which sound they most want to record, and I begin there; I credit every recording to the person who made it, in the listening room and in each print I produce.
[Who gets in] The workshops are free, held at the library after school, and loan every recorder, so no teenager needs equipment or fees. I removed the requirement of an application piece after finding that only teenagers with a prior hobby had signed up, and enrolment from the three nearest high schools rose from 9 to 46.
