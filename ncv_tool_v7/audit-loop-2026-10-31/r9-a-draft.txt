THE CANDIDATE'S BACKGROUND AND SKILLS / PARCOURS ET COMPÉTENCES DE LA PERSONNE CANDIDATE  [first in the CV, written last in the tool]
============================================================
I am a choreographer and associate professor of dance at a Montréal university. My work is in site-specific dance and research-creation with community partners.

For eight years my work has asked who authors dance made in places where people work and live, and what changes when the people who move through a place write its choreography. Three strands carry the question: site-specific dance works made with workers, a public archive of movement scores written by non-dancers, and a community dance program for older adults. Each treats the people who move through a place as its authors. I trained as a contemporary dancer and worked for a decade in repertory companies before a collaboration with a warehouse union local in 2016, where a crew asked me to make a piece about their shifts. That request moved my practice from the stage to the places where people work, and led me back to graduate study in dance and community research. Three works shown at Danse-Cité and in two warehouses, a commission from the Conseil des arts de Montréal worth CAD 48,000, a SSHRC Insight Development Grant (2022), the Prix Opus for the archive in 2025, and a residency at a Berlin dance house in 2023. The research-creation program funds work where making is the method, and my record is built that way: each of my works is the study of its own question, made with the people it is about. A team grant lets me stage the next cycle across three sites at once and keep the archive open to the public. I bring the choreographic method and the access to three warehouse sites. The dramaturg Lucie Fortin contributes the structure and the documentation of process; the sociologist Dr. Imane Belkacem on the team will study how the workers describe authorship; the archivist Sana Haddad keeps the score archive. Each of us holds a part of the question that the others cannot. The next cycle takes the method to a port terminal and a distribution centre outside Québec, and tests whether workers elsewhere write scores that the archive can hold alongside the first 140.

1. Dancing the warehouse: a site-specific cycle made with warehouse workers
   What happens to a warehouse when the people who work in it are invited to move through it as choreographers of their own labour? Dance made for stages seldom meets the bodies that move freight, and warehouse workers are rarely asked how their daily movement could be read as a practice in its own right. I conceived, directed and choreographed the cycle; eleven warehouse workers co-created its movement material, the dancers Amélie Roy and Kofi Mensah performed it, and the dramaturg Lucie Fortin shaped its structure. Over two years I led a series of site-specific works in three distribution warehouses in Laval and Montréal-Nord. We ran weekly movement sessions on shift breaks, built each work from the gestures the workers already used to lift, scan and stack, and staged it in the aisles for coworkers and neighbours. Each work took about eight months from first session to performance. The series comprises three site-specific dance works, "Cadence of the Dock" (2023), "Aisle Nine" (2024) and "Pick Path" (2025), a public archive of 140 movement scores, and a documentary film of the process. The works drew 1,850 audience members across nine public performances, and 62 workers took part in the sessions. Two of the three employers now give paid release time for movement sessions, and the Ville de Montréal archive collection has been taught in two university dance programs. At the last performance of "Aisle Nine", a forklift driver of twenty-two years, Marcel Tremblay, taught the audience the three-beat count he uses to lift a pallet, and the audience moved it with him down the aisle. Marcel and the crew agreed to be named. The movement scores may allow other workplaces and community dance programs to start from what workers already do, and could change how dance made with working people is credited, taught and funded. 2. A public archive of 140 movement scores written for non-dancers
   Can a movement score be written so that someone with no dance training can perform it and credit its authors? Scores usually stay inside companies, and the workers whose gestures they come from are seldom named as authors. I designed the score format and edited the archive; the workers wrote their own scores, and the dramaturg Lucie Fortin and the archivist Sana Haddad built the cataloguing method with me. I ran a series of 36 score-writing workshops in 2024 and 2025, where workers and dancers notated one gesture each as a set of instructions. We tested every score by handing it to a stranger and filming what they did, then revised it with its author, and I edited the final 140 for the archive. A public archive of 140 scores with author credits, a score-writing guide in French and English, and a catalogue with a controlled vocabulary of 52 movement terms. The archive has 6,400 visits in its first year. Four companies and two community centres have staged scores from it, and the guide is on the reading list of one university course. The archive could make workers’ authorship a standard credit in site-specific dance, and give community dance programs a shared repertoire. (a)
   a. Prix Opus, Archive of the Year, Conseil québécois de la musique et de la danse, 2025  [Award / recognition]
      The jury names the archive as a model for crediting community authors.

3. Dancing On, a weekly community dance program for adults over 65
   What does a dance practice look like when it is built around the bodies of people aged 65 to 90, with their own movement vocabulary, instead of adapting a form made for younger dancers? Dance for older adults is mostly offered as exercise, and its participants are rarely treated as artists. I founded the program and lead its artistic direction; the Verdun seniors’ centre hosts it, and four participant-artists co-teach with me. I created the weekly two-hour session in 2022 and taught it for three years in the seniors’ centre, a church hall and a long-term care residence. Each term ends in a shared performance, and the participant-artists choose the movement material. Six terms of the program, six end-of-term performances, a trained group of four participant-artist teachers, and a facilitation guide in French. 118 older adults have taken part, 71 for more than a year. Participants describe the group as the part of their week that they would not give up, and two centres in Laval and Longueuil have started their own groups. Treating older adults as makers could change how municipal dance and wellness programs are funded and evaluated. (a)

[Who gets in] The warehouse workers entered the project as authors, not as subjects: I dropped the audition and the dance-training requirement, paid every worker their shift wage for each session from the first week, and let the crew choose which of their gestures became a score.
[Who else you mentor] I mentor two emerging choreographers outside my department. For one, I read every draft of her first commission application, introduced her to a presenter and sat in on her first rehearsal week at her request; she was funded on the second try.
