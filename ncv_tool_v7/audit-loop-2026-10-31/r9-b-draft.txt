PERSONAL STATEMENT  [first in the CV, written last in the tool]
============================================================
I am a professor of health services research at Northfield University. My work is in diabetes care and community-governed health data, in partnership with the Northern Cedar Health Authority.

Since 2011 my research has taken up one question that the Northern Cedar Health Authority put to me and to its own staff: how can a First Nations health authority deliver diabetes care at home and hold its own records, so that its members and community health workers decide what is learned from them? Three strands answer that question, each led by the Authority. A home-care nursing program that the Authority designed and runs, a diabetes care dataset that the Authority owns and governs, and a course in which community health workers become co-researchers. The thread is that the Authority sets the questions and decides how its records are used. I began as a nurse in a regional hospital, where I saw how many First Nations patients with diabetes arrived late. In 2011 the Authority’s first health director asked me to join a working group, and I have worked within its data governance agreement since 2016. The Authority and I hold a CIHR Project Grant (2021 to 2026) and a provincial health ministry grant, and I chair the Authority’s research ethics and data governance committee’s external advisory panel. The Authority’s nursing director has asked me to speak for the program at two national meetings. A federal health agency cited the Authority’s registry in 2024. I am applying for the CIHR Project Grant to take up a question that the Authority initiated, with the Authority as a named partner. My expertise in health services evaluation, and in working under a First Nations data governance agreement, is what the Authority has asked me to bring to the next phase. The Authority plans to extend home-care nursing to its members with kidney disease, and I will support it in building the registry and in teaching its community health workers to run that analysis.

MOST SIGNIFICANT CONTRIBUTIONS AND EXPERIENCES
============================================================
1. Home-care nursing for people with diabetes, designed and led by the Northern Cedar Health Authority
   In its 2016 community health plan, the Northern Cedar Health Authority asked how nurses could give diabetes care at home in its six member communities, so that people no longer travel four hours to the regional hospital. The Authority set that question and has led the program since it began. The Authority’s nursing director leads the program and its data governance committee decides what is analysed; my part is to support the evaluation design and the analysis, and to train community health workers as co-researchers. From 2017 to 2024 the Authority’s eleven nurses made 3,800 home visits across the six communities. At the Authority’s request, I helped design the evaluation, trained eight community health workers as co-researchers and ran the analyses that its data governance committee approved. The program produced a nursing protocol for home visits, a diabetes care dataset that the Authority owns and holds under its data governance agreement, two reports the Authority approved for release and one paper co-authored with its nursing director. The Authority reports that yearly hospital admissions for diabetes among enrolled members fell from 22 per cent to 14 per cent between 2017 and 2024. Its community health workers now run follow-up visits in all six communities, and the Authority has funded the program from its own budget since 2022. In March 2023, at a community meeting, an Elder described how the visiting nurse had found an early foot ulcer during a home visit, and how the Authority’s wound-care team treated it the next day. The Authority’s council asked that the story be told in these words, with the Elder’s permission. If other First Nations health authorities choose to adopt the model, home-care nursing for diabetes could reach their members as well. The Authority has offered to share its nursing protocol and training materials on its own terms, and three neighbouring authorities have asked to visit. (a, b)

2. A community-owned diabetes care dataset, built and governed by the Northern Cedar Health Authority
   The Authority’s data governance committee wanted to know which supports kept members with diabetes out of hospital, and needed its own records to find out, because earlier hospital data held by the province did not identify its communities. The Authority owns and governs the dataset and its analysts maintain it; the research team advises on its structure and analysis methods at the committee’s request. Between 2017 and 2024 the Authority’s nurses and community health workers entered 5,200 visit records into a registry that the Authority built, and its analysts and the research team cleaned and described them each year. Eight community health workers learned to run the yearly analysis themselves. The registry holds 5,200 visit records for 910 members, with a data dictionary written by the Authority’s analysts, a yearly report that the Authority approves before release and a data governance agreement renewed in 2022. The Authority used the registry to set its own targets for foot checks and eye exams and to report to its members each spring. In 2023 it used the same records to negotiate a nursing budget with the regional health ministry. The Authority has said that its approach to governing its own records could serve other nations that are building registries; whether and how to share it is the Authority’s decision. (a)
   a. 2023 nursing budget agreement between the Northern Cedar Health Authority and the regional health ministry, which draws on the Authority’s own registry; the signed copy is held by the Authority.  [Policy / practice uptake]
      The ministry funded home-care nursing in all six communities on the basis of the Authority’s figures.

3. Training community health workers as co-researchers in six First Nations communities
   In 2018 the Authority’s community health workers asked to learn research methods so that they could analyse their own communities’ records. The Authority’s council approved a training program on that request. The Authority’s council directs the course and its nurses and Elders teach it; I teach research methods and work with each community health worker on the analysis of their community’s records. Over 2019 to 2024 three cohorts of a ten-month course were taught to 24 community health workers by the Authority’s nurses, Elders and the research team. Each worker was supported to analyse records from their own community, and we met every month with the Authority’s council. A ten-month curriculum was designed by the Authority’s nurses and Elders and was approved by its council. Twenty-four community health workers completed it, and nine now analyse their communities’ records as paid staff. Nine of the 24 graduates now hold paid analyst posts with the Authority, and three have presented the analyses of their communities’ records to their own band councils. The Authority has made the course part of its annual training plan. Other First Nations health authorities may adopt the course if they choose to; the Authority has agreed to share its curriculum with two of them from 2026, with the Elders’ approval. (a)
