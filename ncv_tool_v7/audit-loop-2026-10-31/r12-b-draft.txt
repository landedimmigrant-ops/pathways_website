PERSONAL STATEMENT  [first in the CV, written last in the tool]
============================================================
I am a researcher and theatre artist at a Canadian university. My work is in arts-based health research.

What happens to the way people with dementia and the people who care for them talk to each other when they make theatre together, and what that changes in how care is practised. Three strands: participatory theatre-making with people living with dementia, staff-made film in hospital care, and training for the arts facilitators who work in long-term care. The thread is that the people who live and work in care shape the art and the questions. I trained as a theatre director and spent eight years running community drama, then moved into health research after a hospice commission showed me how little time carers had to talk with the person they cared for. The Long Afternoon was performed at Maple Court Care Centre and shown to 120 people; two peer-reviewed articles; a facilitator guide adopted by a regional arts and aging network; a provincial arts council residency; a seed grant for the staff-made film. The Project Grant funds health research that changes practice. I bring the participatory methods and the partnerships with care homes and hospital staff that carry arts-based work into care settings. Next I will test the workshops across eight care homes in two provinces, with the guide as the training standard and carers as co-researchers.

MOST SIGNIFICANT CONTRIBUTIONS AND EXPERIENCES
============================================================
1. A participatory theatre program with people living with dementia and their carers
   How can making theatre together give people living with dementia and their carers a shared way to say what they want to say to each other, when their talk in clinic and at home has narrowed to care tasks and decline? I designed and directed the program as the lead researcher and artist, with a nurse manager and two actors who co-led the sessions. We ran weekly improvised theatre workshops for 24 weeks in three long-term care homes, with carers rehearsing alongside residents, and closed each cycle with a performance for families and staff. Three devised performances, a rehearsal-room guide for facilitators, and two peer-reviewed articles on how the workshops changed the way carers and residents talk. Two care homes added the workshops to their activity calendars, and carers reported using the rehearsal games at mealtimes. At the final performance a resident who rarely spoke sang a verse she had improvised, and her daughter joined her on stage. Other homes could run the sessions with their own staff, which would change what dementia care programming offers families. (a, b)
   a. Hartwell, L., and Okafor, N. (2026). Shared stages: devised theatre in long-term care. Arts and Health Quarterly, 14(2), 101-118.  [Peer-reviewed]
      Describes how carers and residents began to talk to each other during the workshops.
   b. The Long Afternoon, devised performance with residents, carers and two actors, Maple Court Care Centre, 14 March 2026, 120 attendees.  [Exhibition, performance or screening]
      Families and staff attended; the care centre filmed the performance for its own training use.

2. A film made with hospital staff about caring for patients with dementia on acute wards
   What does it feel like to care for a person with dementia on a busy acute ward, and how can staff show each other that experience when it rarely fits into handover notes or training slides? I directed the film and led the workshops that shaped it, with four ward nurses and a porter as co-creators of the scenes. I ran ten storytelling and filming workshops over five months with 14 hospital staff on three wards, and the staff chose, wrote and acted the scenes. A 22-minute film, a discussion guide for ward teams, and a conference presentation at an arts and health meeting. The hospital shows the film in its dementia-care induction, and 90 staff have watched it in facilitated sessions so far. Other hospitals could use the film and guide in staff training, which could change how acute-care teams talk about dementia at handover. (a)
   a. Interview on the film, Arts and Health Radio, 5 May 2026, with the hospital education lead.  [Media / public]
      The interview describes how the staff chose the scenes.

3. A training guide for arts facilitators working in long-term care
   How can an artist with no clinical training lead a creative session safely and well for residents who have dementia, when most facilitator training covers either arts practice or care but not the two together? I wrote the guide as lead author and edited the contributions of five facilitators and two care-home managers. I gathered practice notes from 31 sessions, ran three review workshops with facilitators and care staff, and tested drafts of the guide in four care homes over eight months. A 64-page open-access guide in English and French, with a one-day training outline and a consent and safeguarding checklist. Two arts organizations now use the guide to train their facilitators, and 46 facilitators have completed the one-day training. Provincial arts councils and care-home networks could adopt the checklist for the facilitators they fund, which could make arts programs in care homes safer and easier to start. (a)
   a. Adoption letter from the Northern Arts and Aging Network, 2 June 2026, naming the guide as its facilitator training standard.  [Policy / practice uptake]
      The network trains its facilitators from the guide.
