PERSONAL STATEMENT  [first in the CV, written last in the tool]
============================================================
I am a nurse scientist at a mid-sized Canadian university. My work is in harm reduction and community-based overdose response.

For ten years my research has asked how overdose response can be shaped by the people who live it, and what changes in health services when people who use drugs set the priorities and own the data. Three strands address it: a co-designed outreach program, a community overdose-surveillance dashboard and peer-worker training. In each, people with lived experience set priorities and share authority, and the connecting thread is shared decision-making over how services and data are used. I trained as a public health nurse and worked in outreach for eight years before returning to graduate school, when peer workers told me that research about them rarely reached the services they used. That background is why every project I lead begins with a partner agreement. I hold a CIHR operating grant and a provincial health research award, and the dashboard and outreach schedule are now used by two public health units and one regional health authority. Peer-council members are co-authors on two of my papers. The Project Grant funds the next phase of this work, in which the peer council and I will test the outreach and dashboard together in three more health regions, and the design of the work depends on a multi-year partnership. The next phase moves the outreach schedule and the partner data agreement into health regions that already fund peer work, with partners deciding in each region what is shared.

MOST SIGNIFICANT CONTRIBUTIONS AND EXPERIENCES
============================================================
1. Harm reduction outreach designed with people who use drugs
   Peer workers and outreach staff told us that existing overdose-prevention supplies and hours did not match when and where people used drugs, and asked for a program shaped around their own priorities. I co-led the program with a peer-worker council of eight people with lived experience, who set its priorities and decided how it ran. Over 22 months, with a peer-worker council, a harm-reduction nonprofit and two public health units, we co-designed a mobile outreach program and ran it in three neighbourhoods, with 1,900 contacts recorded by peer workers. The work produced a peer-led outreach protocol, a peer-worker training curriculum used with 64 workers, a supply-distribution schedule set by the council, and two papers, one of them co-authored with three council members. Two public health units and one regional health authority have adopted the outreach schedule, and the council now holds a standing seat on the authority overdose working group. At a March 2025 council meeting, a peer worker presented the outreach schedule to the regional health authority board, which approved it that week with no changes. The program could move into other health regions that already fund peer work, and the council and regional authorities decide jointly whether and how it does. (a, b, c)

2. Peer-worker training grounded in what peer workers asked to learn
   Peer workers carry much of the front-line overdose response, and they asked for training that recognizes their expertise and offers paid, certified learning. I contributed the curriculum evaluation, while an experienced peer worker and a harm-reduction educator led the content and delivery. Between 2022 and 2025, I worked with three peer educators and a college continuing-education unit to adapt and teach a 5-day curriculum to 64 peer workers across six cohorts, and gathered their feedback after each cohort. The work produced a certified training curriculum with a facilitator guide, a short course record that peer workers can add to their own resumes, and one practice brief written with peer educators. Two health authorities now pay peer workers for training time, and the college offers the curriculum each term with graduates teaching later cohorts. Other health regions could offer the curriculum, and peer-worker networks and employers would decide whether and how to adopt it. (a)
   a. Two regional health authority policies that fund peer-worker training time, 2024 and 2025  [Policy / practice uptake]

3. A community overdose-surveillance dashboard that partners can read and use
   Health authorities reported overdose data months late and in forms that neighbourhood groups could not use, so community responders asked for local counts they could act on within the week. I led the dashboard design and evaluation, while the harm-reduction partners chose which indicators to show and own the data-sharing agreements. In 2023 and 2024 we worked with four community organizations and one regional public health unit to build and test a weekly dashboard, and held 14 working sessions where partners reviewed every indicator and its wording. The dashboard covers 11 neighbourhoods, uses a data-governance agreement written with the partners, and has an open data dictionary and one paper describing how it was built together. Four organizations check the dashboard each week to plan outreach shifts, and the public health unit now publishes the same local counts monthly. A peer outreach worker used the Friday dashboard counts to move the weekend van to a different block, and reported reaching 31 people that night who had not been reached the week before. Other regions with community partners could use the same governance agreement, and each partner group would decide whether its data is shared. (a, b)
