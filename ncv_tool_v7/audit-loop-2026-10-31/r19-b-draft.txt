PERSONAL STATEMENT  [first in the CV, written last in the tool]
============================================================
I am a professor of biomedical engineering at a research-intensive Canadian university. My work is in wearable sensors and clinical device engineering.

For 20 years my research has addressed one problem: clinicians make treatment decisions from glucose readings that are hours old, and a wearable measurement they can trust at the bedside would let them act in time. Three strands address it: sensor and signal-processing design, open firmware and calibration methods that other groups can build on, and training for the clinical engineers who validate and maintain the devices. All three make the measurement dependable where it is used. I came to biomedical engineering from electrical engineering and spent four years at a device company before returning to the university. That background is why my work starts from what a hospital can verify and a regulator can approve. My group holds a CIHR Project Grant and an NSERC Alliance grant with the device company, and has attracted 6.2 million dollars in cooperative funding over ten years. The sensor is licensed to the company and sold in 14 countries, the firmware toolkit is used by seven research groups, and I chair the national working group on wearable glucose monitoring standards. I received a society award for translational engineering in 2022. The Project Grant funds the clinical accuracy and adoption work that follows a working prototype. My next phase moves the sensor from insulin-infusion wards to general medical wards, where the same device can prevent unseen lows in patients on oral agents, and this competition supports a trial of that size. The next phase extends the sensor to general medical wards and to community pharmacies, adds a second analyte through the same firmware interface and carries the training program to six more hospitals.

MOST SIGNIFICANT CONTRIBUTIONS AND EXPERIENCES
============================================================
1. A wearable glucose sensor that clinicians use at the bedside
   People with type 1 diabetes still prick a finger up to eight times a day, and ward nurses in hospital have no continuous glucose reading for patients on insulin infusions, so overnight lows go unseen for hours. I led the sensor and signal-processing design for a team of nine and wrote the trial protocol with two endocrinologists. Over six years my group built five sensor generations with a medical-device company, which supplied manufacturing and regulatory support, and tested the final one in a 212-patient trial at two hospitals, comparing readings with laboratory glucose every hour. The work produced a granted patent licensed to the company, a registered clinical trial, three peer-reviewed articles, an open firmware toolkit with 14 releases, and a clinical-engineer training manual. The hospital network now uses the sensor on its insulin-infusion wards, and the number of overnight lows below 3.0 mmol/L that go unseen fell from 31 per 100 patient-nights to 9. On one night shift in March 2024 a ward nurse acted on an alert at 3 a.m. and corrected a low reading of 2.6 mmol/L before the patient showed symptoms. If the accuracy holds in the other hospital networks that have asked for it, continuous bedside glucose monitoring could replace hourly finger-prick checks on insulin infusions across Canadian wards. 2. A training program for clinical engineers
   Hospitals adopt connected medical devices faster than their clinical engineers can learn to validate, maintain and troubleshoot them, so devices sit unused or are used without checks. I founded the program, wrote the curriculum with the hospital's clinical engineering department and teach the two core courses. Since 2013 we have run an annual twelve-week program combining device design, regulatory requirements and supervised work on hospital wards, with 148 participants from 19 hospitals and 11 device companies. The program produced a training manual in its fourth edition, two course modules adopted by a professional association and a competency checklist for device validation. Of the participants, 92 now work in clinical engineering departments and 41 in device companies, and two provincial health authorities use the checklist when they approve new devices. If the checklist is adopted as a common standard, device validation could follow the same steps in every Canadian hospital. (a, b)

3. An open firmware toolkit for wearable biosensors
   Each research group that builds a wearable sensor writes its own firmware for sampling, calibration and wireless transfer, so results from different devices cannot be compared and student projects restart from zero. I designed the toolkit's calibration architecture and review the contributions from outside groups. With four graduate students I wrote the sampling and calibration layers, ran bench validation on 38 sensor units across five hardware platforms, and set up a public repository with automated tests that run on each submission. The work produced 14 tagged releases since 2020, a specification of the calibration interface, a methods paper and a library of 22 documented test cases. Seven research groups in four countries and the device company's test laboratory now build on the toolkit, and the company replaced its own calibration code with it in 2024. If the interface becomes the common one, results from different wearable devices could be compared directly and new groups could start from validated code. (a, b)
