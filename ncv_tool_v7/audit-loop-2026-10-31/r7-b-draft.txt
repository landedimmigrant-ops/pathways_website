PERSONAL STATEMENT  [first in the CV, written last in the tool]
============================================================
I am an assistant professor of history at a mid-sized Canadian university. My work is in digital history and the computational analysis of historical newspapers.

For more than a century, historians have studied wars through the newspapers of the time, yet nobody could read a whole press at once, so the question of how everyday language prepared readers for war rested on the few articles each scholar happened to read. Three strands address it: text-mining methods that detect shifts in vocabulary, the digitized newspaper corpora those methods need, and historical studies of wartime press language. The thread is that each method is checked against close reading of the sources. I came to this work from archival history. Reading microfilm for my dissertation showed me how much a single reader misses, so I taught myself to program and then trained in text analysis through a two-year collaboration with a computer scientist. That background is why every tool I build is tested against the sources a historian would check. I have released one open-source tool used at seven universities, built a corpus of 310,000 newspaper pages with the university library, and co-authored an article in a leading Canadian history journal. A provincial graduate scholarship supported my doctoral work, and a software company now builds on the tool through a partnership agreement. Insight Development Grants support new questions and methods at an early stage. My next project tests the text-mining method on French-language papers with a partner library and needs one team and two years to do so. The next phase extends the method to French- and Ukrainian-language newspapers and releases a second version of the tool with the software company, so that historians of other regions can run the same comparisons on their own collections.

MOST SIGNIFICANT CONTRIBUTIONS AND EXPERIENCES
============================================================
1. An open-source text-mining tool for reading changes in wartime newspaper language
   Historians who study wartime newspapers read a few hundred issues by hand, so claims about how language shifted across a whole press rest on small samples and on whatever each reader notices. I led the method and wrote most of the code; developers at a software company built the interface and the library team hosted the data. Over three years I built a pipeline that detects shifts in word use across 1.2 million newspaper pages, checked it against a hand-coded sample of 4,000 articles and ran four workshops in which historians applied it to their own collections. The work produced an open-source Python package under an MIT licence, a documented method and a browser-based tutorial. Historians at seven universities have installed the package, and two regional archives now run it on their own newspaper holdings. At a 2025 workshop an archivist ran the package on her own collection and found a change in how the paper described conscripts that her catalogue had not recorded. If the method holds in other languages and presses, historians could test claims about public opinion across a whole national press. (a)
   a. Northpress Reader 1.2, open-source Python package for tracking vocabulary change in newspaper corpora (MIT licence; archived at Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.0000000)  [Dataset / tool]
      Seven universities and two archives use it; the 2025 release added a tokenizer for French-language papers.

2. A digitized corpus of 140 years of newspapers from northern Ontario, built with the university library
   Most northern Ontario newspapers survive only on microfilm that cannot be searched, so historians of the region and the communities that read those papers cannot see how local life was reported. I directed the project with the library, chose and sequenced the titles, and designed the OCR correction workflow that library staff and five student assistants carried out. Between 2021 and 2025 we scanned 310,000 pages from 22 papers, corrected the OCR text with a crowd-assisted workflow, tagged named places and tested search quality on 600 hand-checked pages. The project produced a public corpus with 310,000 pages of searchable text, a documented metadata schema and a data paper in a humanities journal. The library now hosts the corpus, and local historical societies and two school boards have used it for exhibits and classroom units on the region. If other libraries adopt the workflow, regional newspaper collections that sit on microfilm could become searchable at a fraction of current cost. (a, b)
   a. Northern Ontario Newspapers Corpus, 310,000 pages of OCR text with place tags and metadata, hosted by the university library (a digitized collection)  [Dataset / tool]
      Researchers and school boards searched it more than 40,000 times in its first year.
   b. Okafor, A., and Lindqvist, M. 2025. A corpus of northern Ontario newspapers, 1880 to 2020. Journal of Digital Humanities Methods 6(2): 112-134.  [Peer-reviewed]

3. A co-authored article that shows how wartime newspapers changed their words for conscription
   Historians of the First World War home front have argued about conscription coverage from selected headlines, so the shift in everyday language across a whole press had never been measured. I wrote the argument and the analysis sections, and my co-author, a political historian, wrote the background and the discussion of sources. We applied my text-mining method to 90,000 issues from 14 Canadian papers between 1914 and 1919 and read 300 passages the method flagged to test each finding against the sources. The work produced a peer-reviewed article in a humanities journal, with an open replication notebook. The article has been cited by 11 later studies and is assigned in two graduate courses on the Great War home front. If the method is applied to later conflicts, historians could compare how press language prepared readers for each war. (a, b)
   a. Okafor, A., and Brandt, S. 2024. Conscription in the headlines and in the columns: measuring press language in Canada, 1914 to 1919. Canadian Historical Review 105(3): 401-432.  [Peer-reviewed]
   b. Cited in 11 later studies of war-time press language, including two doctoral dissertations.  [Replication / citation]
