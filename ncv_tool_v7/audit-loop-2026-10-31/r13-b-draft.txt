PERSONAL STATEMENT  [first in the CV, written last in the tool]
============================================================
I am an associate professor at a Canadian university. My work is in computer science, in the fairness of automated decision systems.

For eight years my research has addressed one question, how to measure whether an automated hiring tool treats groups of applicants differently, in a form that employers, vendors and applicants can all check. Three strands address it: a statistical method for auditing ranking models, an open-source toolkit that puts the method in the hands of audit teams, and a licensed patent that lets a vendor build the method into its product. The thread is that every audit has to produce evidence that someone outside the research group can verify. I came to this work from machine learning for recommender systems, and a collaboration with two sociologists showed me that a model can pass every accuracy test and still treat groups of people differently. That is why my methods start from the question a hiring committee would ask. I hold an NSERC Discovery Grant and an NSERC Alliance Grant. The toolkit I lead has been downloaded more than 8,000 times, and a patent from this work is licensed to a startup. A paper from my group received a best-paper award in 2025, and the work has been used by an employer and by two public agencies. The Alliance program funds research that is carried out with partners who can use the results, and sustained partnership with employers and agencies is where my record is strongest. I am applying to extend the audit method to hiring decisions made with several systems at once. The next phase tests whether audits remain valid when an employer changes its screening model during a hiring season, and it will give audit teams a way to compare results across two or more systems.

MOST SIGNIFICANT CONTRIBUTIONS AND EXPERIENCES
============================================================
1. Auditing hiring algorithms for fairness
   Automated hiring tools rank applicants for large employers, and neither the employers nor the applicants can tell whether the rankings treat groups of applicants differently. I led the design of the audit method, including the statistical tests for group differences in rankings, and I wrote the first version of the software. Under a confidentiality agreement with a large employer that cannot be named, my group developed statistical tests that detect differences in how a ranking model treats groups of applicants, and applied them to the employer's screening models. We also interviewed hiring managers and recruiters to see how the rankings are used. The work produced two methods papers, an open-source auditing toolkit that has been downloaded thousands of times from a public package index, and a patent on the auditing method that is licensed to a startup. The employer changed how it audits its screening models and adopted the toolkit in its review process, and a startup now sells audits built on the licensed method. Researchers at other universities have used the toolkit in their own audits. In a review meeting at the partner employer, the human resources team used the audit report to pause a screening model until a ranking gap between two groups of applicants was explained. If regulators and employers adopt routine audits of automated hiring tools, applicants could be assessed by systems whose group-level effects are measured and reported before the systems are used. 2. An open-source toolkit for fairness audits
   Researchers and employers who wanted to audit a hiring algorithm had to write their own statistical tests, so audits were rare and could not be compared from one system to the next. I set the design of the toolkit and wrote its core modules; two graduate students added the reporting tools and the documentation. We packaged the tests from the audit method as a library with a command-line tool, wrote tutorials, and ran workshops for researchers and for employers' analytics teams. Releases follow a public issue tracker, and each one is tested on synthetic applicant data. The work produced one open-source software package with three major releases, a tutorial series and a benchmark of synthetic applicant data. The toolkit has been downloaded more than 8,000 times, and audit teams at two public agencies and one nonprofit now use it to report group differences in their own screening models. At a workshop for analytics teams, a participant ran the toolkit on a live screening model and found a ranking gap the team had not seen. If audit tools become a routine part of procurement, buyers of hiring software could compare vendors on the same measures before they sign a contract. (a, b)

3. A patented bias-detection method, licensed to industry
   A fairness test that stays in a paper does not reach the vendors who build hiring software, and vendors had no legal route to use a university method inside a product. I invented the method with a doctoral student, wrote the claims with the university's technology transfer office, and negotiated the licence terms with the startup. We filed a patent application on the detection method, tested the method inside the startup's product on synthetic applicant data, and interviewed six vendor teams about how a licensed audit would fit into their release process. The work produced one granted patent, one exclusive licence for hiring applications, and one set of integration notes for the startup. The startup now offers audits built on the method to employers in two sectors, and the licence income supports maintenance of the open-source toolkit. If vendors build audits into their products, employers could receive group-level results with each tool they buy, and applicants could rely on a shared standard of evidence. (a)

      The licence is the first the university has granted for a fairness method.
