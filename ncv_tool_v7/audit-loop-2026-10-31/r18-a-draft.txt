PERSONAL STATEMENT  [first in the CV, written last in the tool]
============================================================
I am an associate professor at a mid-sized Ontario university. My work is in the sociology of education, with a focus on how schools sort students.

For ten years my research has asked how the courses a student is placed in at fourteen shape the adult that student can become, and why the same placement falls hardest on students from low-income and racialized families. Three strands address it: a longitudinal study of course placement across four Ontario cohorts, a public dashboard built with three school boards, and a teacher-education module that teaches candidates to read placement data. The thread is that the sorting of students is a set of ordinary decisions, and decisions can be changed once the people who make them can see their pattern. I taught Grade 9 in a school that tracked students before I returned to graduate school, and the first student I could not move out of applied courses is why my analysis begins with the placement meeting. I hold a SSHRC Insight Grant and a Connection Grant, and I built the first linked dataset of course placement in Ontario, which four other research teams now use. Three school boards have signed standing agreements to keep the dashboard running, and the provincial ministry asked for my analysis in 2023 and 2024. The Insight Grant funds sustained inquiry that is designed with the people who run the systems it studies. That is how this work has been built since 2019, and partnership with school boards is where my record is strongest. The next phase follows the cohorts into early adulthood to see where placement in Grade 9 leads by age 25, and extends the dashboard to postsecondary admissions data.

MOST SIGNIFICANT CONTRIBUTIONS AND EXPERIENCES
============================================================
1. Tracking students out of academic courses in Ontario high schools
   Students in Ontario enter Grade 9 and choose, or are placed in, applied or academic courses, and the placement strongly shapes who can reach university. Families in lower-income neighbourhoods and Black and Indigenous students are placed in applied courses more often, and no one has followed how those placements hold over the four years. I co-lead the study with two colleagues; the three school boards set which board-level questions the dashboard answers, and I designed the linkage of course placements to credit accumulation. From 2018 to 2024 we followed four cohorts of Grade 9 students, 41,000 in all, through administrative records from three Ontario school boards, and held 36 interviews and 12 focus groups with students, parents, guidance counsellors and teachers. The boards helped us choose the sites and read the findings before release. The study produced six peer-reviewed articles, a public dashboard of course placement and credit accumulation for the three boards, and a 40-page plain-language report for families. Two of the three boards changed how students are counselled into Grade 9 courses after they saw the dashboard, and one board ended its early course-selection form in 2023. In October 2023, a guidance counsellor in one board used the dashboard in a meeting with a Grade 8 family and moved their daughter into academic English after seeing that students with her marks were placed there at twice the rate in neighbouring schools. The provincial ministry, which decides whether course-selection policy changes, and the other boards in Ontario could use the dashboard to see who is placed in applied courses before Grade 9 begins. Counsellors in participating schools also asked for the linked records to guide placement meetings with families, a use that depends on each board agreeing to release them. (a, b, c)

2. How families and counsellors decide on Grade 9 courses
   Policy debates assume that families choose courses freely, but little is known about how counsellors frame the choice for families who have less time, less English or less trust in the school. I led the interview study and trained two graduate students to code the transcripts. Over 2019 and 2020 we interviewed 58 families and 24 counsellors in 11 schools, observed 17 course-selection meetings and followed 19 families through their Grade 9 choices. The study produced three journal articles, a doctoral dissertation and a brief for parents in five languages. The parent brief is now handed out at course-selection nights in two boards, and a school council in one board asked for the interview findings to guide its meeting. The provincial parent association could distribute the brief, and counsellors could use the meeting observations as a basis for professional learning. (a, b)
   a. Ramos, L. and Chen, A. (2022). How counsellors frame the Grade 9 course choice. Sociology of Education, 95(3), 201-219.  [Peer-reviewed]
      Describes the scripts counsellors use with families.
   b. Radio interview on course selection and equity, CBC Metro Morning, September 2023.  [Media / public]

3. A public dashboard that lets school boards see who is placed in which courses
   School boards hold course-placement and credit data but rarely look at it by neighbourhood or student group, so the patterns in who is placed in applied courses stay hidden from the people who set counselling practice. I led the dashboard design with three board data analysts, who owned their boards data and set what could be shown. Between 2021 and 2023 the research team and the analysts at three boards met monthly for 22 months to agree on indicators, privacy rules and plain-language labels, and tested drafts with 14 counsellors. The work produced an open dashboard with nine indicators, a data-sharing agreement template and a short guide for board staff. Two boards now review the dashboard at their yearly counselling meetings, and the template was adopted by a fourth board in 2024. At the March 2024 counselling meeting of one board, the system principal for student success used the dashboard to show principals that Black students in two schools were placed in applied courses at nearly three times the board rate, and asked each school to explain its placement practice. The ministry and other boards could adopt the same indicators, and parents could use them if the boards decide to make the dashboard open to families. (a, b)

   b. Data-sharing agreement template, signed by three boards in 2022 and used by a fourth in 2024.  [Community document]

4. A teacher-education module on how course placement reproduces inequality
   New teachers rarely learn how the placement decisions they take part in sort students by income and race, so the practices this research describes continue unexamined in the schools where graduates start work. I designed and teach the module, with a former guidance counsellor and a curriculum specialist who led the case materials. From 2022 to 2025 we built a six-week module around the project findings, taught it to 210 teacher candidates in three cohorts, and revised it after each cohort with student and associate-teacher feedback. The work produced a six-session module with case studies drawn from the three boards, an instructor guide and a short open-access article on the module design. Two other faculties of education in Ontario now use the case studies in their courses, and 38 of the 61 graduates who answered a follow-up survey said they had changed how they talk to families about course choice. In a 2024 follow-up interview, a first-year teacher described stopping a colleague from steering a student into applied mathematics on the basis of a single test, and showing the Grade 8 marks of similar students that the module had taught her to compare. The module could become part of the provincial teacher-education standards if the college that accredits programs adopts it, and the instructor guide could support in-service workshops for counsellors.
