PERSONAL STATEMENT  [first in the CV, written last in the tool]
============================================================
I am a marine biologist at a mid-sized Canadian university. My work is in kelp-forest ecology and fisheries science.

My research program asks how quickly coastal kelp forests recover from marine heatwaves, and what that recovery time means for the fisheries that depend on them. Three strands address it: a long-term canopy monitoring record, an eDNA method for mapping juvenile lobster habitat and a training course for fisheries technicians. All three turn coastal observations into numbers that managers can use. I came to this work from a fisheries technician post on the Scotian Shelf, where I saw how little recovery data managers had. Between 2021 and 2026 I took a parental leave of 12 months and a medical leave of 8 months and moved my lab between universities. My team kept the monitoring record running through both leaves, and the record carries wider uncertainty for the two seasons sampled at reduced effort, which I report in every analysis. I hold an NSERC Discovery Grant and a partnership agreement with a provincial fisheries association. My canopy dataset is open and is used by two other research groups. My output count for 2021 to 2026 is lower than in the preceding five years, a difference that matches the 20 months of leave I took in that period, and the citation count for the 2024 recovery-model paper is 31. The Discovery Grant funds a long-term program, and a six-year record with two documented gaps is the kind of program it can support, because the design lets me state exactly how much each gap costs. The next phase extends the canopy record to four more reefs and tests whether eDNA can replace diver counts in the thinly sampled seasons.

MOST SIGNIFICANT CONTRIBUTIONS AND EXPERIENCES
============================================================
1. Six years of kelp forest recovery data, kept going through two leaves
   Kelp forests along the Scotian Shelf support lobster and urchin fisheries, and the fishers who depend on them have had no multi-year baseline for how quickly canopies return after a marine heatwave. I started the monitoring program in 2021 and led it, then handed field sampling to a postdoctoral fellow during my two leaves in 2023 and 2025 and resumed leadership on my return. Over six years we surveyed 48 permanent transects at 12 sites. During my leaves in 2023 and 2025 the summer dive surveys ran at reduced frequency, so we filled those seasons with the fisheries partner's trap-camera footage, and I rebuilt the sampling design on my return so that the gaps can be modelled. The program produced an open six-year canopy dataset, a recovery-time model that carries wider intervals for the two reduced-sampling seasons, and three papers. The fisheries association now uses the recovery estimates to time its urchin-removal permits, and it has asked for the model to be rerun each autumn. Because two seasons were sampled thinly, we report those estimates with wider intervals, and the association's managers say they prefer that to a gap. In October 2025 a harvester on the association's board used the recovery curve at a public meeting to argue for a two-year pause on urchin removal at one reef. If the regional harvest plan adopts the recovery-time estimates, they could guide urchin removal across the Scotian Shelf, and the gap-tolerant design may help other monitoring programs that have interrupted records. 2. An eDNA method that finds juvenile lobster habitat from one water sample
   Fisheries staff locate juvenile lobster habitat with diver surveys that take a crew three days per reef, so most reefs go unsurveyed. I co-led the method development with a colleague at my former university and moved the work to my new lab in 2024, where I rebuilt the validation set. Between 2022 and 2025 we collected 310 water samples at 18 reefs and matched each to a diver count. The lab move in 2024 interrupted the sequencing for eight months, and I now test for a batch difference between the two labs in every model. A validated eDNA assay for juvenile lobster, a reference library of 64 sequences, an open protocol and two papers. Two provincial fisheries units adopted the protocol in 2026 and now screen 40 reefs a season in place of diver surveys, which frees roughly a third of their dive-crew days for stock assessment. If the assay is adopted nationally, it could let fisheries managers map nursery habitat for the whole Atlantic coast within one field season. (a)
   a. Okafor, Lindqvist and Tran (2026). A single-sample eDNA assay for juvenile American lobster. Methods in Ecology and Evolution.  [Peer-reviewed]

3. A field course that trains fisheries technicians in kelp and eDNA sampling
   Fisheries technicians collect most coastal monitoring data but receive little formal training in sampling design, which lowers the value of the records they produce. I designed the curriculum and taught the first two cohorts; a colleague now teaches it while I am on sabbatical. We ran five-day courses for 36 technicians from four provincial and community fisheries organizations, with two days of fieldwork on a working reef and a practical test of each person's sampling plan. The course produced a 120-page manual, a set of sampling templates and a certification checklist. Graduates now collect the partner organizations' monitoring data, and two organizations report that their records pass the provincial data-quality audit for the first time. If the certification checklist is accepted by the provincial regulators, it could become the standard for coastal monitoring records in the region. (a)
