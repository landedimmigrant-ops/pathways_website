PERSONAL STATEMENT  [first in the CV, written last in the tool]
============================================================
I am an assistant professor of history at Fictional University. My work is in labour and community history in Ontario mill towns.

Histories of working towns rest on the records that owners and officials kept, while the workers’ own records are held by families and local societies that have few means to preserve or share them. I read company records against the press to count what the owners’ accounts left out; I help local historical societies catalogue and digitize the records they hold; and I curate public exhibitions with the families whose members appear in those records. One thread connects them: the records of a working town belong to the town. I grew up in a mill town and trained as a labour historian; my doctoral research took me into a company’s ledgers, and I came back to work with the historical society that held them. A 2025 article on the 1912 Harlow Falls strike in the Journal of Canadian Labour History, a digitized collection of 21,400 pages with 5,200 visits, a public exhibition seen by 3,100 people, and a monograph under contract. An Insight Development Grant funds the stage I am at: testing the ledger method in two more mill towns with their historical societies before a larger application. Next I will read the ledgers of two more mill towns with their historical societies and write the monograph’s comparative chapter.

MOST SIGNIFICANT CONTRIBUTIONS AND EXPERIENCES
============================================================
1. Reading a 1912 mill strike through the company’s own ledgers
   Histories of Ontario mill towns have told the 1912 Harlow Falls strike from newspaper reports written by the owners’ allies, and the company ledgers that record who was paid, fined and dismissed had never been read against them, so the workers’ side of the strike had no documented count. I wrote the chapter as sole author, working from ledgers the Harlow Falls Historical Society lent me. I read the mill’s payroll ledgers for 1905 to 1919 and matched 1,310 wage entries to the strike committee’s list of 212 dismissed workers. I then set the ledger record against three newspapers and the provincial labour inspector’s report. A 9,000-word chapter, “Paid in scrip: the Harlow Falls strike of 1912,” in my monograph under contract with Fictional University Press (manuscript due January 2027), and a peer-reviewed article of the same title in the Journal of Canadian Labour History 91 (2025), 55–82. The Ontario Labour Museum now uses the 212-dismissal count on its strike panel, and two graduate seminars at other universities assigned the article in 2026. In March 2026 a descendant of one dismissed worker found her great-grandfather in the ledger and corrected the family’s account of why he left Harlow Falls. If mill-town ledgers elsewhere were read this way, historians could rebuild who was dismissed in strikes that the press covered from the owners’ side. (a, b)

2. Opening a mill town’s archive to researchers as a searchable digital collection
   The Harlow Falls mill archive sat in 140 uncatalogued boxes in a church basement, so no researcher outside the town could learn what it held, and damp was damaging the earliest ledgers. I designed the cataloguing scheme and the digitization plan, and nine Historical Society volunteers did the scanning under my training. I catalogued the 140 boxes at item level, trained 9 volunteers to scan to archival standard, and transcribed the 1905 to 1919 payroll ledgers with them over 18 months. A digitized collection of 21,400 pages with item-level descriptions and full-text transcriptions of the payroll ledgers, deposited in Fictional University’s open repository and linked from the Historical Society’s website. The collection has had 5,200 visits since June 2026, and the Provincial Archives accepted the original ledgers for conservation in September 2026. If other small-town societies adopted the cataloguing scheme, mill archives elsewhere could be opened to researchers before damp or fire destroys them. (a, b)

3. A public exhibition that returns the mill’s records to the town
   Most residents of Harlow Falls had never seen the mill’s records or heard the strike told from the workers’ side, and the town’s history was taught from a plaque that names the owners only. I curated the exhibition with the Harlow Falls Historical Society, which chose the objects and wrote the family panels with me. I selected 60 ledger pages and photographs with the society’s curators, wrote the panel text in plain language, and ran 4 community days where families added their own objects. A 10-week exhibition, “The Ledger and the Line,” at the Harlow Falls Public Library in spring 2026, with 14 panels and a walking tour guide for 12 sites, and a school kit of 6 lesson plans. Over 3,100 visitors attended, the library kept the panels as a permanent display, and the Harlow Falls school board added the school kit to its Grade 10 history course in September 2026. At the third community day, a retired mill worker identified her mother in a 1912 strike photograph, and the society added her account to the display. If other historical societies used the same approach, small-town museums could show records from the workers’ side as a regular part of their displays. (a, b)
